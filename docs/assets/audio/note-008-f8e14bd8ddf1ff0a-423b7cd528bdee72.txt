Before the click, a stamp needs a footprint you can inspect. Every proposed cell belongs to the preview. If one part would leave the island, meet an occupied cell, hit the height limit, or cost more material than you have, the whole proposal must remain a proposal. Without that boundary, a click can leave two placed pieces and one unexplained gap. The player has to discover what happened, count what was spent, and repair a shape they never asked to build. The source validates the complete footprint before spending or placement. It also includes rollback paths for spending and placement failures. That is the machinery behind a simple expectation. After the click, a successful multi-cell stamp goes into history as one batch. One undo attempts to remove that batch and refund its materials. The source checks that every target still has the expected block at its top before removal. If those checks fail, it reports undo failed and keeps the history entry. It does not promise that the world can always be wound backwards. That limit matters. A reassuring label should not turn a changed world into somebody else's cleanup job. There are tradeoffs. A bounded stamp is quicker to preview and easier to understand than a free-form blueprint editor. It also offers fewer shapes. Grouping the gesture makes reversal coherent, but undoing one piece within the group remains a separate editing task. Something worth trying again. The hypothesis is that a player will explore more freely when the preview, material cost, and reversal all describe the same gesture. We have not measured that with players here. A useful next observation is small. Ask someone to place a three-cell run, reject a blocked proposal, and undo a successful one. Before they press undo, ask what they expect to disappear.
