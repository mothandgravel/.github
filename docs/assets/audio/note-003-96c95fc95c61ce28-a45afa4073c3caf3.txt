You come back to a project after a day away. Some work has landed. A decision has changed. Someone needs an answer. Before doing anything else, you need to recover the shape of what happened. Decision Docket is our own tool in production for our own work. It is concerned with that return, finding the reason behind a decision and continuing with the relevant context. The reader still needs titles, dates, and source context to understand what it means. One distinction matters especially. The past and the present need different places. Selecting Friday's history should never make today's unanswered question disappear. A beautiful filter would be a poor one if it taught a person that the work no longer needed them. This is where a visual metaphor earns its place. It helps someone orient themselves, then makes the relevant detail easier to reach. Shape carries meaning. Words keep that meaning accountable. The native experiment compares river, circle, and focus using the same synthetic changes and questions. Focus offers one change at a time with explicit previous and next controls alongside its pager. Giving each direction the same work makes the comparison more useful. On a phone, a gesture has neighbors. A drag through the river lives beside vertical scrolling and the system's back gesture. A question sheet lives beside the keyboard, rotation, and the possibility that the app goes into the background. Those are design materials too. The prototype therefore separates looking, choosing, and committing. Swiping changes the visible item. Selecting an answer prepares a choice. An explicit action commits it. Pending, unknown, and recorded results have distinct presentations because they describe different things the person needs to understand. This is a native experiment, not a silent replacement of the web reply behavior. The phone needs its own account of interruption and return. The useful part has to survive the spectacle. A reduced motion setting should keep every selection and every question. Large text should have room to breathe. A list or a labeled day control should let someone complete the same task without learning the drawing's gestures. There is still work to do.
