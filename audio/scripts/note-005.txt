Moth and Gravel. Field Note 5. A green light should mean something.

An AI-narrated edition of the published note.

What, exactly, is all right?

A green light is wonderfully efficient. One glance, one small exhale. The thing is all right. You can turn your attention elsewhere.

But what, exactly, is all right?

As we work across our software pipeline, that small question keeps returning. A process is running. A job has ended. A checked plan has passed its expected case. Each deserves a clear answer. Each asks something different.

The colour can travel more easily than the meaning.

Start with the question

Geaidnu is being developed as a calm workspace companion. It brings the current issue, repositories, services and evidence into one place. Its contract makes an important distinction: a healthy runtime is an observation about the runtime. Feature acceptance needs its own evidence.

That distinction belongs in the interface. A person should be able to see a service running without having to infer that the intended work has been verified.

The contract also keeps a failed runtime probe unknown. An observation that did not succeed cannot establish that an application stopped. This is a small wording problem with a large consequence: the interface has to leave room for what it could not learn.

01 / OBSERVATION

Is it running?

A runtime observation answers a question about the service’s present state.

02 / EXPECTATION

Did the case behave as expected?

A case can pass by correctly refusing a step or preserving an unknown outcome.

03 / EVIDENCE

Is the result established?

Delivery needs evidence of its own. Missing observations remain visible.

Three questions to keep distinct. Explanatory drawings, not screenshots or a proposed shared product interface. The labels summarize the documented boundaries described in this note.

A passing case can preserve a question

Ajo checks agent plans and records their execution. Its local integration report contains a useful wrinkle: every acceptance case in the final run passed, while the delivery records still included incomplete, unknown and unresolved outcomes.

Those outcomes can be correct. A case designed to refuse an unsafe next step passes when the refusal holds. A case designed to preserve a lost acknowledgement passes when the missing answer remains missing.

The local integration report records six verified delivery subprobes separately from its 34 passing cases. The distinction is the story. It lets a reviewer understand both the mechanism’s behavior and the work actually established.

These are different boundary fixtures, not a sample from which to calculate a general success rate. A tidy summary must give the reader a way back to that difference.

The boundary can be modest

Paja, a build tool for spare machines, gives green a narrow job: every configured step exited with zero. Its result file records step outcomes, and typed statuses distinguish a failed step, an infrastructure failure, cancellation and timeouts.

That is a useful boundary because it can be explained. The person can inspect the recipe and decide whether its steps answer the question they care about. The colour does not have to carry an unspoken claim about every possible property of the software.

It also matters where the verdict comes from. Paja uses the recorded result and exit codes, rather than discovering success in an encouraging sentence buried in a log.

Give uncertainty a place

The design challenge is to make these distinctions available without turning every surface into a report. A concise state, a clear description of what was observed and a route to its evidence can do a great deal.

Unknown needs a place of its own. So does a refusal that prevented a mistaken action. Neither should require a person to guess whether the tool has lost track of the work.

Across these projects, the question is becoming more specific: what can this indicator honestly let someone stop worrying about?

That is the promise a green light should keep.
