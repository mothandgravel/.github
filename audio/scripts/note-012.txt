Moth and Gravel. Field Note 12. The picture stays.

An AI-narrated edition of the published note.

We stopped the local server. The coastline stayed where it was. So did the last vessel positions.

The picture still had its shape. Helsinki above, Tallinn below, a few marks in the water between them. There was plenty left to look at. The connection message had changed, and the age beside the selected observation kept growing.

That quiet interval became the useful part of Coastal watch.

A coastline lends conviction

Coastal watch began with a maritime dashboard in Raivari’s defense concept. A track plot needed a place to belong. We gave it a basemap, then worked on selecting a mark and keeping its record close.

The coastline helped immediately: a position could be read in relation to a shore. Zooming gave the view a different scale. Selecting a track linked the mark to a source, a time and a short history.

It also made the whole thing look more settled. A recognisable coast gives a drawing some borrowed authority. The land can be real while every vessel on it is synthetic. In this study, that is exactly the arrangement.

Finishing the picture gave us another obligation: make its limits as easy to find as its details.

There is more than one clock

A message has an observation time. The dashboard also has a time when it received that message. Those are two useful pieces of information.

A recently received message can describe an older position. A connected stream can carry an ageing observation. The reassuring word “connected” answers a question about the connection; the mark still needs a time of its own.

We kept observation age and receipt age separate. The selected record puts the source and observation age beside the position and movement. You can look into one mark without giving up the surrounding water.

That is a small arrangement of labels. It changes what the reader has to remember.

Let the gap become visible

The server has stopped. The last position remains, its age continues to grow, and the interface offers a reload. Actual capture from the synthetic rehearsal. See the interruption in the short film

Removing every mark when the connection drops would remove useful context too. Leaving the picture untouched would make the gap hard to see.

In the prototype, the last position stays. The connection state changes. The age continues to count, even though no new message arrives to prompt it.

The retained position now has a more modest job: this is where the last observation put the vessel. The screen has no new observation to offer.

We made the interruption by stopping the local server. The recording shows that behaviour. It does not tell us whether someone arriving halfway through the gap would understand it.

Keep the record within reach

A mark is good at attracting attention. Its record gives that attention somewhere to go.

The reported identifier, source, age and recent positions belong together. A quality figure needs its own explanation: the demonstration heuristic in this dashboard cannot establish a vessel’s identity or intent. Zooming closer cannot supply that missing evidence either.

This is where polish becomes more than a surface. The selection colour needs to connect the chart and the record. The words need to say what was observed. The older position needs to remain recognisably older.

Ask someone to read the silence

The next useful study is quite plain. Show someone the interrupted view, without explaining it first. Ask where the selected vessel is, how they know, and what they would want to check next.

Listen for the difference between “it is here” and “it was last seen here”. Then ask them to find the source and the age. A label can be present without doing its work.

We have not run that observation yet. For now, Coastal watch gives us a working picture and a gap we can repeat. Enough to put a better question in front of someone.

The water stays on the screen. The account of it needs to keep its time.
