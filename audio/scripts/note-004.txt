Moth and Gravel. Field Note 4. A control should stay where you left it.

An AI-narrated edition of the published note.

The button was there a moment ago

You have found the next action. Your eyes leave the text and your hand starts toward the control. Then a picture finishes loading. The card grows, the words move down and the button is somewhere else.

Nothing dramatic has happened. The page has not failed. Yet it has asked you to find your place again.

Decision Docket, our upcoming product being developed under Navivra, had a small version of this problem in its teammate card. A decorative portrait appeared only after its local image had loaded. Until that moment, the grid contained no portrait row.

The loaded card looked right. The journey to that appearance needed attention.

Make room before the picture arrives

The approved portrait was already 64 by 64 pixels. Its arrival added that height and the existing eight-pixel gap. In controlled measurements of the actual component, the following text and Switch control moved by 72 pixels.

The fix was a permanent decorative wrapper in the portrait’s existing position. It reserved the same 64-by-64 space from the start. The image still appeared only after loading; the card simply knew where it would belong.

No extra artwork was needed. No animation was added. The surrounding type, spacing and control could remain as they were.

The same-role pending-to-loaded positions stayed fixed at both tested widths.

Explanatory diagrams, not application screenshots. The figures summarize recorded component measurements at 390 and 1440 pixels for Product Partner and Engineering Lead. Pale dashed marks show the earlier position or reserved space.

An empty space can be the right answer

While the portrait is pending, its place stays blank. If decoding fails, it stays blank. The existing error handling prevents a broken image from taking its place.

That might seem like an invitation to add a shimmer, a spinner or a message. Here, the image is decorative. The role and its description are already expressed in text. The person can read the card and use its control without waiting for the portrait.

A loading treatment would make the missing decoration more prominent. A quiet reserved space gives the rest of the card permission to carry on.

There is a useful distinction here. A missing piece of information may need an explanation. A missing decoration may only need room.

Keep the same role in view

The card lets someone switch between Product Partner and Engineering Lead. An uncached role can need another image load. During that transition, the old portrait must not masquerade as the new role.

The existing cleanup and loading behavior were retained. The reserved slot does not introduce a new focus target or an announcement. In the bounded keyboard walks, focus stayed on the real Switch control while the role changed and the portrait loaded or failed.

The text can still reflow when the role changes. Different descriptions may have different lengths, especially on a phone. The promise is narrower: loading a decorative portrait for the same role should not rearrange the work beneath it.

Measure the interruption you meant to remove

The comparison mounted the actual card with its real local artwork, styles and fonts in an isolated fixture. Delayed image responses reproduced the original movement for both roles at desktop and phone widths. After the change, the measured displacement was zero in all four comparisons.

The loaded arrangement also retained the same measured geometry. This matters: making the pending state stable did not require a different finished card.

Failures and cleanup received their own checks. The appearance review covered pending, loaded and invalid-image states across the two product themes and light and dark appearances, with a reduced-motion case. Those observations establish a small component behavior. They do not establish whole-page layout stability or show how a real person experiences the full onboarding flow.

That is enough to make this change worth recording. An interface contains many small invitations to spend attention. Keeping a control where someone has already found it is one way to ask for less.
